For an independently walking 80-year-old with knee osteoarthritis

Which powered walking aid is worth trying?

The first decision is whether the device makes her actual painful activities easier. A powered hip frame may help with fatigue and hills, while a powered knee device is more directly aimed at stairs and getting up from a chair. Neither has been shown to treat her arthritis.

What we know about her

She can walk and her movement control is reasonably good; the main concern is arthritic knees. Before buying, identify whether pain is worst on level ground, stairs, slopes, or rising from a chair, and whether she has had falls or knee buckling.

What the research can—and cannot—tell us

Promising early results

A 2024 systematic review found a growing but varied evidence base for lower-limb exoskeletons in older adults. A 2026 feasibility study in ten older adults reported a 13.6% reduction in walking metabolic cost with a research hip exosuit. These are not tests of the retail products above.

Knee arthritis evidence is narrower

A trial of Keeogo in knee OA and a four-person pilot of a research knee exoskeleton are relevant to the condition, but do not establish that DNSYS Z1, Exo Motion or Hypershell will reduce her pain. Published evidence should be matched to the specific device before drawing that conclusion.

Important correction to the earlier recommendation

Less walking effort does not necessarily mean less knee joint pain or a lower fall risk. A hip motor can change gait and loading in ways that vary by person. The clinical studies above do not verify the marketing percentages for any device sold here. Do not treat a product listing, certification claim, or ARTG listing as proof of arthritis benefit.

How to run a useful trial

  1. Ask her physiotherapist or treating clinician which knee movements should be avoided or tested, especially if she has buckling, recent falls, swelling or recent surgery.
  2. Record a baseline: comfortable walking distance, pain rated 0–10, a few safe steps or stairs, and getting up from her usual chair.
  3. Check height, weight, waist and leg measurements, ability to put the device on, strap pressure over the painful knee, and comfort while sitting.
  4. With a trained person nearby, repeat her usual activities with assistance on and off. Stop if pain, instability, skin pressure or unusual gait increases.
  5. Confirm total delivered cost, used-device return conditions, batteries, servicing and local warranty before purchasing. Online return windows may be less useful once a device has been worn.

If pain is the main barrier

Ask a clinician about usual knee OA management, exercise therapy and whether an unloading brace is suitable for her pattern of arthritis. A conventional brace may be more useful than a powered hip device for some knees. Keep a walking stick or prescribed aid if one is needed; these consumer exoskeletons do not replace balance support.
